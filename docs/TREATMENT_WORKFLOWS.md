# Orthodontic workflows for lecture demonstrations

Researched 22 September 2026. These are teaching outlines for three distinct mechanisms, not treatment protocols or patient recommendations. The order is a useful classroom narrative; actual care varies. No forces, activation turns, wire dimensions, wear schedules, treatment durations or prescribed movement amounts are supplied.

## What the animation can explain

An appliance transmits mechanical loading to living tissues. Orthodontic tooth movement involves the periodontal ligament (PDL), cell signalling, bone resorption and bone formation. A primary mouse study directly implicated PDL fibroblasts and osteoblast-lineage signalling in movement and osteoclast activity; its experimental results are not a human movement prescription. [Jeon et al., 2021, primary experimental study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33852725/)

Forma currently applies authored geometric transformations to meshes. It does not solve a force system, tissue remodelling, a centre of resistance, sutural opening, root resorption or biological timing. A tooth moving after a wire appears in a lesson communicates the sequence of events, not a computed response to that wire. Stages are narrative checkpoints, not treatment visits or elapsed time.

Three labels should remain distinct:

| Label | What changes clinically | What the teaching display may show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed-brace tooth movement | Tooth position and orientation through the supporting tissues | Brackets, engaged archwire, schematic load arrows and authored tooth poses |
| Tooth-borne palatal expansion | Intended maxillary transverse change, with variable skeletal and dentoalveolar components | A palatal appliance and clearly labelled conceptual skeletal/dental components; no predicted sutural response |
| Dental archwire expansion | Dental arch dimensions through tooth repositioning/inclination and associated supporting-tissue response | Buccal movement and possible tipping; do not depict the palatal suture opening as a result of ordinary archwire expansion |

The University of Alberta randomized comparison of Hyrax expansion and a self-ligating archwire system found differing transverse dental/alveolar effects and buccal crown tipping. This supports distinguishing the mechanisms; it does not establish a universal skeletal-to-dental ratio or a recipe for every appliance. [Nam et al., 2020, randomized clinical trial](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/9/10/3167)

## Workflow 1 — Fixed brackets and archwire

The NHS describes bracket bonding, wire engagement and ligatures as separate fitting components. Brackets couple the wire to teeth; ligatures retain the wire. Do not equate the coloured ligatures with all the forces involved in treatment. [NHS: Braces](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/braces/), [Frimley Health NHS: Fixed appliance components](https://www.fhft.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-library/care-fixed-orthodontic-appliances)

| Step | Installed / action | Intended effect | Limit to explain | Discussion question and answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Define the starting problem | Show initial crowns, schematic roots and the bite; no appliance yet. | Identify alignment, inclination, spacing and inter-arch relationships. | A surface model cannot establish periodontal health, root anatomy or a complete diagnosis. | **Why inspect more than the visible crown row?** Root position, supporting tissues and the bite constrain what an apparently straighter row can mean. |
| 2. Bond brackets | Add brackets at illustrative crown locations; keep tooth positions unchanged. | Provide attachments through which the appliance can act. | Bracket position affects mechanics. The app's estimated placement is not clinical bonding guidance. | **Do bonded brackets alone straighten the teeth?** Their role is attachment; the engaged appliance supplies the mechanical loading. |
| 3. Engage the archwire | Show a flexible archwire retained by modules/ties, or explain a self-ligating clip. | Connect teeth through the selected wire/bracket system. | A line drawn between slots is only a visual connection. It does not calculate wire deflection, force or friction. | **What do the coloured rings do?** They secure the archwire to brackets; they are not equivalent to separate inter-arch elastics. |
| 4. Explain alignment and tissue response | Animate a deliberately authored alignment example; optionally show PDL/bone labels. | Explain how sustained loading can lead to supporting-tissue adaptation and tooth movement. | Motion is neither instantaneous nor a rigid tooth sliding through unchanged bone. The animation calculates no cellular response. | **Why can the wire not determine movement by geometry alone?** Mechanical loading interacts with living tissues and their biological response. |
| 5. Refine position and inclination | Illustrate later clinician-selected wire control and adjustments; compare rotations and bite. | Explain refinement beyond simply lining up crown tips. | Wire sequence, engagement and adjuncts vary; a thicker wire is not automatically better or appropriate. | **Is a visually smooth arch sufficient?** No. Tooth inclination, root relationships, contacts and tissue limits also matter. |
| 6. Review and remove the active appliance | Compare initial and illustrative final positions; then hide wire and brackets. | Separate active correction from the post-appliance phase. | A satisfactory screen image does not establish a completed or stable clinical result. | **Does appliance removal make the result permanent?** No. Retention is a distinct part of the treatment pathway. |
| 7. Retain and review | Show a passive retainer concept at the illustrated final position. | Maintain corrected positions while tissues adapt and against later change. | Retainers need suitable use, hygiene and review; neither fixed nor removable retention is maintenance-free. | **Why retain after the teeth look straight?** Supporting tissues adjust, and relapse and age-related positional changes remain possible. |

Flexible wires are commonly used early, with different wires introduced as treatment progresses; this is a general description rather than one mandatory material/size sequence. [East Lancashire Hospitals NHS: Fixed Braces](https://elht.nhs.uk/application/files/1615/2362/7703/Fixed_Braces.pdf) Biology in step 4 is supported by the [Jeon primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33852725/). The need for an initial assessment and records is described by [East Sussex Healthcare NHS](https://www.esht.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/0438.pdf); retention and its limits are described by [BOS](https://bos.org.uk/patients/treatments/faqs/).

## Workflow 2 — Tooth-borne palatal expansion

Use a **Hyrax-type tooth-borne example**, not a generic device representing every expander. The conceptual assembly is posterior tooth anchorage, a palatal framework and a central expansion screw. Tooth-borne means load is transmitted through teeth; it does not mean the intended effect is exclusively dental. [North Cumbria Integrated Care NHS: Rapid maxillary expansion](https://www.ncic.nhs.uk/patients-visitors/patient-information-leaflets/orthodontics-rapid-maxillary-expansion)

| Step | Installed / action | Intended effect | Limit to explain | Discussion question and answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Distinguish skeletal and dental narrowing | Show an upper-arch transverse relationship and explain skeletal maturity; no device yet. | Introduce why the location of the discrepancy matters. | Crown width alone cannot determine skeletal deficiency or predict sutural response. | **Is a narrow-looking tooth row enough to select a palatal expander?** No. Dental inclination, jaw relationships, supporting tissues and maturation need distinction. |
| 2. Fit the tooth-borne device | Add illustrative posterior bands/anchorage, framework and central screw. | Establish a pathway for transverse loading through the anchor teeth. | Device design and anchorage vary. The drawing is not an installation plan. | **Why call it tooth-borne if bone change is intended?** The teeth are the load-transfer anchors; the intended response can include the maxillary skeleton. |
| 3. Explain activation | Show the screw concept and opposing transverse arrows without an activation count. | Explain that activation loads the appliance and connected tissues. | A screw displacement is not equal to a known amount of sutural or dental change. | **Does one amount of device opening give the same amount of bone widening?** No. Appliance deformation and dental, alveolar and skeletal responses contribute differently. |
| 4. Separate the possible responses | Use separate labels for skeletal widening, alveolar response and dental tipping. | Explain the mixture of effects rather than only moving crowns laterally. | Response is variable; do not imply a fixed ratio, perfectly parallel opening or symmetry. | **Can molar crowns move buccally without equivalent skeletal widening?** Yes. Dental inclination and alveolar effects contribute to the measured arch change. |
| 5. Review the response | Compare upper/lower transverse relationships, anchor-tooth inclination and tissues. | Explain clinical monitoring while the intended change develops. | An anterior gap may appear, but its size is not a calibrated measure of skeletal widening. The app cannot monitor tissues. | **What else matters besides a larger intermolar distance?** The bite, tooth inclinations, tissue condition and skeletal versus dental contributions. |
| 6. Hold the achieved position | Leave the device present but depict it as passive; stop widening arrows. | Explain stabilization after the active expansion phase. | Passive holding is not continued activation and is not an instant guarantee of stable new tissue. | **Why leave the device after active widening stops?** It can support the achieved position during stabilization. |
| 7. Transition and retain | Explain possible later tooth alignment and appropriate retention; do not impose a universal next appliance. | Distinguish transverse correction from comprehensive alignment and maintenance. | Expansion does not automatically resolve every malocclusion or remove the need for retention. | **Does a wider upper arch mean all orthodontic treatment is finished?** No. Alignment, bite coordination and retention are separate considerations. |

The general placement–activation–review–hold sequence is described by the [AAO palatal-expander guide](https://aaoinfo.org/whats-trending/palatal-expander/) and [North Cumbria NHS](https://www.ncic.nhs.uk/patients-visitors/patient-information-leaflets/orthodontics-rapid-maxillary-expansion). The NHS page contains device-specific home activation instructions; they are deliberately **not reproduced** in this teaching outline.

A randomized study found dentoalveolar tipping with both studied tooth-supported palatal devices, with a difference between their designs. Do not teach pure skeletal expansion or transfer its exact results to every patient/device. [Kılıç, Kiki and Oktay, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276928/)

**Maturation boundary:** sutural morphology and resistance vary between individuals. Chronological age is not a universal open/closed switch. Angelieri and colleagues' observational classification showed overlapping maturation stages across ages; it is not a rule that every adult suture is fused or a recommendation to obtain imaging for everyone. [Angelieri et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24182592/) The AAO distinguishes growing-patient expansion from adult approaches; skeletal anchorage or surgical approaches are different workflows and are not chosen by this lesson. [AAO guide](https://aaoinfo.org/whats-trending/palatal-expander/)

## Workflow 3 — Dental archwire expansion

This means an intended change in **dental arch form** with a fixed appliance. It must not be presented as an equivalent substitute for every skeletal expansion problem. Ordinary dental archwire mechanics do not warrant an animation or claim of predictable midpalatal-suture opening. [Nam et al., primary comparison](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/9/10/3167)

| Step | Installed / action | Intended effect | Limit to explain | Discussion question and answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Define the dental example | Show initial posterior crown inclination and arch width; distinguish it from a skeletal example. | Establish whether the lesson illustrates a dental form change. | A scan cannot establish the available periodontal envelope or make a treatment choice. | **What is the first distinction?** Moving teeth within their supporting structures is different from intentionally widening the maxillary skeleton. |
| 2. Attach the fixed appliance | Add brackets and posterior attachments; no central palatal screw. | Provide coupling between wire and teeth. | Bracket design alone does not determine a unique amount or mechanism of expansion. | **Are self-ligating brackets a skeletal expander?** No. A clip is a wire-retention mechanism, not proof of skeletal expansion. |
| 3. Engage a selected arch form | Show an engaged archwire and conceptual buccal load directions. | Explain how selected wire form and engagement can influence tooth position. | A wider drawn wire is not a dosage or guarantee that supporting bone will follow without limits. | **Why does wire form matter?** It contributes to the appliance's mechanical interaction with bracket positions, alongside material, dimensions and engagement. |
| 4. Illustrate dental change | Show buccal tooth displacement with an explicitly optional tipping component. | Demonstrate a possible increase in dental arch width and change in inclination. | Real movement involves PDL/bone response; app translations/rotations are authored, not predictions. | **Does wider crown spacing prove bodily translation?** No. Tipping can increase crown-level width while root movement differs. |
| 5. Discuss support and inclination | Compare crown and schematic root orientation, without declaring a safe boundary. | Emphasize the relationship between dental movement and supporting tissues. | Expansion is bounded by individual anatomy and periodontal considerations; the app has no patient bone envelope. | **Can the model be expanded indefinitely because the meshes do not collide?** No. Collision-free geometry says nothing about periodontal feasibility. |
| 6. Coordinate and compare | Compare initial/final dental arch form and upper/lower relationships. | Explain that alignment and transverse changes interact with the bite. | Changes vary by region and individual; no fixed total width or uniform all-tooth displacement follows from this lesson. | **What should the comparison be called?** A dental arch-form change, not measured skeletal palatal expansion. |
| 7. Retain the dental result | Show a passive retention concept after active tooth movement. | Explain maintenance and the possibility of later positional change. | A geometric endpoint cannot establish stability or replace retainer review. | **Why retain a change that was “only dental”?** Tooth-supporting tissues and other influences continue to affect position after active movement. |

A multicentre randomized trial found no significant bracket-type effect on transverse or inclination changes when the groups used the study's common wire sequence. This argues against teaching special expansion powers based only on bracket branding. [Fleming et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23910199/) Original clinical research also demonstrates that alveolar support can change during alignment; it does not validate a universal safe expansion amount. [Nakawah et al., 2025, randomized clinical trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11818706/)

## Retention: the common final discussion

Retention is a phase of care, not an assertion that biology has stopped. Removable and bonded retainers have different practical requirements. Long-term relapse/change can remain possible, and bonded retainers need inspection and hygiene. Avoid animations that imply a retainer actively locks the bone forever or that removing braces alone guarantees stability. No wear schedule is supplied here. [British Orthodontic Society: retention FAQ](https://bos.org.uk/patients/treatments/faqs/)

For palatal expansion, distinguish **passive stabilization with an expander** from later **tooth-position retention**. They address related but different portions of the workflow. The passive device should remain visible without continued expansion arrows. [AAO phases](https://aaoinfo.org/whats-trending/palatal-expander/)

## Engineering implications for the teaching app

These are design recommendations inferred from the sources, not additional clinical claims:

- Keep “installed appliance”, “intended effect” and “authored geometric example” visible as separate concepts in each step.
- Show brackets before wire engagement without moving teeth. Show a retainer/passive expander without active load arrows.
- Keep skeletal and dental expansion diagrams distinct. If the app only has crowns and gums, a palatal lesson must explicitly say skeletal response is discussed but not simulated; moving the crowns alone cannot substantiate it.
- If a schematic split palate or suture is added, label it conceptual. Its separation, symmetry, gap and timing must not appear to be patient-specific predictions.
- Use original/current overlays to support discussion, not as evidence of treatment success. Root illustrations, appliance positions and load arrows are schematic.
- Offer replay, pause and next/previous narrative steps. Avoid labels such as “week”, “dose”, “turn”, “safe expansion” or “recommended treatment” for generated frames.
- Keep quantitative movement bounds described as software controls. The numbers used to make classroom changes visible are not force prescriptions or biologically safe limits.

Only official professional/hospital education and original research were used as clinical sources. No commercial product page, unlicensed textbook download or patient case advice was used to define these workflows.
